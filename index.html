Section 0Building an Operator

Start from the person, not the dice. You'll never roll for a stat, never trade a good number for a skill, never wait to find out who you are. Ten steps, one sitting, and the character you pitched is the character on the sheet.

The Shape Of It

You pick a Tradition (your profession: it sets your numbers, your class feature, your Hope Feature, and the two domains you draw cards from) and a Path within it (your specialty inside that profession). You pick an Origin (where you grew up, and what you were) and decide whether you're Augmented or Pure Human. You place a fixed array of trait modifiers, write two Experiences, take two domain cards, take your gear, and connect to the crew.

Step 2 · Tradition And Path

Your Tradition is your class. Your Path is your specialty within it: a Solo is a Hitter or a Bodyguard, a Hacker is a Runner or a Deep Seer, a Nomad is a Coyote or a Fabricator. Each Path has three features. You start with the Foundation. At tier 2 you can spend an advancement to take the Specialization, and at tier 3 another to take the Mastery.

Step 3 · Origin

Your Origin is two picks: where you grew up, the part of the city (or the road) that raised you, and what you were, the life you had before you started taking jobs. Pick one of each. Together they give you three features that are always yours, whatever your Tradition.

Step 7 · Starting Loadout

Everyone Starts With
  • One primary weapon and, if it's one-handed, one secondary weapon, from the Tier 1 rows in Section B.
  • One armor from the Tier 1 rows, or a Light Frame from Section D if your character is clan or clan-adjacent.
  • One Tier 1 augment (Augmented) or one clan kit item (Pure Human), plus the free interface or block.

  • A stack of dollars (about $1,000: two medpatches, a burner phone, and walking-around money), and one personal item that matters.

Step 9 · Experiences

An Experience is a word or phrase that says something specific about your operator. Each starts at +2. Before any action or reaction roll, you can spend a Hope to add a relevant Experience to the result, and more than one if more than one fits. Keep them specific enough to mean something and broad enough to use: Ex-Corp Security beats Good At Fighting; Dr. Lobotomy is a great Experience for the person carrying the arm sword. Every Tradition page suggests six. You gain a new one at levels 2, 5, and 8, and you can rewrite one with the GM whenever it stops fitting. Experiences say who you are; Paths and cards say what you can do.

Step 10 · Your First Two Cards

Choose two level-1 cards from your Tradition's two domains: both from one domain, or one from each. Cards are the specific things you're good at, and they're where two operators of the same Tradition stop resembling each other. The full decks are in Section A, after the Tradition pages. You'll add one card every level, and by level 5 you'll have more cards than you can hold at once; the loadout rules are in Section A.

Leveling Up

The whole party levels together at milestones the GM calls. Level 1 is tier 1; levels 2 to 4 are tier 2, 5 to 7 are tier 3, 8 to 10 are tier 4. When you level up:

  1. Tier achievements. At level 2, 5, and 8: add a new Experience at +2 and gain +1 Proficiency. At 5 and 8, clear the marks on your traits so you can raise them again.
  2. Choose two advancements from your tier's list below, or from any earlier tier. Each has limited picks per tier; tick the box.
  3. Take a domain card of your level or lower from one of your domains.

Section 0 · OptionalConversions

A Conversion is a fundamental change in what your character is: an engram in a borrowed body, a mind with company, a chassis instead of a skeleton. Each one is a real benefit paired with a real burden. They are optional, one per character, and the GM hands them out as the story does, or offers one at character creation if the concept calls for it.

How Conversions Work

A Conversion is a card on your sheet that doesn't count against your loadout. It sits with your Origin and your Path. Taking one means taking its drawback too, so remind the GM of it when it matters. A Conversion never stops you taking damage or making death moves. Nothing here is a humanity cost. Augmentation is capability; a Conversion is a situation, and situations can be resolved.

Conversion · 01

Sleeved

You are an engram running a body that isn't the one you were born in. Maybe yours died. Maybe it was sold. Maybe you were never anything but the copy.

Features Unfinished Business. Work with the GM to decide what you are still here to do. When it's done, you choose: pass into the net for good, or stay in the sleeve and give up this card for a new Experience at +3.
Data First. Your engram is the real you and the body is a peripheral. You have advantage on rolls to resist quickhacks, engram edits, and anything that targets a mind. Once per long rest, when you mark your last Hit Point, you can mark 2 Stress to be already backed up: the sleeve dies, and you wake in the net, a fridge, or another body the GM names, with 1 Hit Point and all your Stress marked.
The Burden. The sleeve's augments are the sleeve's; they stay with the body. The body has a warranty and a previous owner. It reads as them to every scanner, its augments are theirs, and once per session the GM can spend a Fear to have someone recognize the face.

Questions: Whose body is this, and who else knows? What memory did the transfer lose? Who has the other copy?

Conversion · 02

Full-Body Conversion

A chassis, not a skeleton. Your brain and spinal column, and a great deal of Militech-grade engineering.

Features Chassis. Gain +3 to both damage thresholds and +1 Armor Score, and you're immune to poison, gas, and less-lethal weapons. You count as a machine for quickhacks: neural loads work on you, and so do device quickhacks.
Won't Stay Dead. When you choose Risk It All and fail, you can permanently mark a Hit Point slot to succeed instead; you still use the Hope Die's value to clear Hit Points and Stress. When you permanently mark your last slot, the chassis is done.
The Burden. During a rest, you can clear Hit Points only with parts: a Ripper, a Fab Bench, or salvage from a wreck of your tier. Describe the repair. No parts, no healing. Every ripper in the city knows what you are on sight.

Questions: Who paid for the chassis, and what do they think they own? What did you keep of the old body? What does the maintenance cost you that isn't money?

Conversion · 03

Passenger

There is a second engram in your head. A construct, a copy, a dead friend, a stranger, a corporate asset that got loose. It has opinions.

Features The Passenger. Write their name on your sheet and work with the GM on their sphere: what they know and want (Violence, Money, the Net, the Old Days, Revenge, Faith, the Road). Before an action roll in their sphere, you can spend a Favor to roll your Passenger Die (d6; d8 from level 5) and add it.
Favor. Start with 3. Use a downtime move to give the Passenger something they want (a visit, a message, a body count, a screen) and gain Favor equal to your Presence. When you succeed with Hope, you can take a Favor instead of a Hope.
The Burden. When you fail with Fear, the Passenger can act: the GM narrates one thing you did or said that you didn't choose. When your Favor is 0, the GM can spend a Fear to have the Passenger take the spotlight in your body for one action.

Questions: How did they get in? What do they want that you can't give them? Who would pay to have them extracted, and what would be left of you?

Conversion · 04

Prototype

You are a corporate asset. Something in you is worth more than you are, and someone holds the paper.

Features Gifted. Gain +1 to action rolls, reaction rolls, and damage rolls.
The Burden. When you fail a roll, mark a Stress or the GM gains a Fear, on top of any Fear the roll already gave them. The corp that made you knows where you are to within a district, and once per session the GM can put someone in the scene who's there to check on the investment.

Questions: Which corp, and which division? What do the tests measure? What happens when the numbers stop going up?

Conversion · 05

Combat Chassis

Military wetware with a mode you didn't ask for. When the body decides it's a fight, it stops asking.

Features Overdrive. When you mark 1 or more Hit Points, you can mark a Stress to go into Overdrive. While in it, gain a bonus to attack rolls equal to your tier and a 1d6 bonus to damage rolls. When you roll with Hope in Overdrive, you must mark a Stress. It lasts until you rest or until Burnout.
Burnout. When you mark your last Stress in Overdrive, the system dumps: roll a number of d20s equal to your tier and deal that much physical damage to everything within Very Close range, then drop out and mark a Hit Point. Choose what it hits only if you have Hope to spend: 1 Hope per creature spared.
The Burden. Someone is hunting the model. The hardware is Militech property, and it phones home when Overdrive ends.

Questions: Who installed it, and did you agree? What did Overdrive do the first time? What do you do to keep it from starting?

Consent, Removal, And What This Is Not

Talk about Conversions at Session Zero. They change a character's story hard, so they're player-chosen or player-agreed, never sprung. Every one of them can be undone in play: an engram can be moved, a Passenger extracted, a chassis bought out, a prototype's paper burned. That's a job, and jobs are what this game is made of. What a Conversion is not is a judgment on how much chrome is too much. There is no such number here.

Section RThe Rules in Play

The Dice

Every action roll is two d12s: a Hope Die and a Fear Die in different colors. Add them together, add your trait modifier, add any Experience you spent a Hope on, add or subtract an advantage or disadvantage d6, and compare the total to a Difficulty. Then look at which die was higher.

Advantage adds a d6 to the total; disadvantage subtracts one. They cancel one for one. Reaction rolls (dodging a blast, resisting a quickhack) use the same dice but generate no Hope or Fear.

Hope and Fear

Hope is yours. You start each campaign with 2, hold up to 6, and spend it to: add an Experience to a roll (1 each), Help an Ally (1, they gain an advantage die), initiate a Tag Team roll with another PC (3, once per session), power your Tradition's Hope Feature (3), or power a card (Section A). Scars cross out Hope slots permanently.

Fear is the GM's. They gain one whenever you roll with Fear, plus a handful at every rest, and they hold up to 12. They spend it to interrupt you, to spotlight extra adversaries, to trigger an adversary's or environment's Fear feature, or to add an adversary's Experience to a roll. The GM also spends Fear when security steps up. If a SkyPark drone fleet goes from Alert to Maximum Enforcement, that's the GM spending Fear, and you'll know it happened.

Heat

Your crew has one Heat score, 0 to 10. It rises when you kill city cops or civilians, use explosives in public, get identified on a quickhack against corp staff, or go viral. It drops when you lay low, pay someone off, do a job for whoever you angered, or beat whoever comes after you. At the start of every session the GM's Fear pool starts at the number of PCs plus half your Heat, rounded up. Above Heat 6, adversaries hunting you gain an Experience (Hunting the Crew +2). At Heat 0, nobody is looking for you, and people are happy to hire you.

Combat

No initiative, no rounds, no fixed action count. The spotlight moves to whoever the story points at. When you act, you can move within Close range as part of it; moving farther, or through danger, is an Agility Roll. When you roll with Fear or fail, the spotlight goes to the GM, who makes a move: one adversary acts, and each Fear spent spotlights another. Then it comes back to you. An exchange is one pass of the spotlight: an action and the GM's answer to it.

Attacks

An attack is an action roll with your weapon's trait against the target's Difficulty (adversaries) or Evasion (you). On a success, roll damage: a number of your weapon's dice equal to your Proficiency, plus the weapon's modifier. On a critical, start from the maximum of the dice and add a normal roll.

Taking Damage

Compare the damage to your thresholds, which come from your armor plus your level. Below Major, mark 1 Hit Point. At Major, mark 2. At Severe, mark 3. Before you mark, you can spend one Armor Slot to drop the damage one step (Severe to Major, Major to Minor, Minor to nothing). Your armor has slots equal to its Armor Score; they come back when you repair during a rest.

Stacking

Your Armor Score is your worn armor's score plus at most 2 from every other source combined (augments, accessories, frame components, Path features). A shield’s Barrier counts with your armor, not against the 2. A bonus to the same trait or to Evasion from gear and augments doesn't stack: only the highest applies. Bonuses from advancements and Path features always apply.

Massive Damage

Damage at double your Severe threshold marks 4 Hit Points. Some hits are just that bad.

Stress

Six slots to start. You mark Stress to power cards and gear, to accept a complication the GM offers, and when adversaries rattle you. When your last Stress is marked you become Vulnerable until you clear one. If you must mark Stress with none left, mark a Hit Point instead.

Less-Lethal

Any weapon that could plausibly subdue (batons, fists, stun rounds, most crowd-control kit) can deal its damage as Stress instead of Hit Points: one Stress per Hit Point the damage would have marked. A target with no Stress left marks Hit Points as usual. Corporate security in Chicago is briefed for arrests; expect the baton before the bullet.

Rests and Downtime

A short rest is an hour off the street: the van, a safehouse, a back room. Each PC takes two downtime moves: tend to wounds (clear 1d4 + tier HP, yours or an ally's), clear Stress (1d4 + tier), repair armor (1d4 + tier slots), or prepare (gain a Hope; 2 each if you prepare together). Three short rests, then the next must be long. A long rest is a night somewhere safe: you choose two moves, and each long-rest move clears everything of its kind (every Hit Point, every Stress, or every Armor Slot) or gains 2 Hope. Instead of a move, you can work on a project: a clan-fab build, an augment installed at a ripper, a dive arranged. Projects run on a countdown.

The GM gains 1d4 Fear on a short rest and 1d4 plus the number of PCs on a long rest. The city keeps moving while you sleep: every faction has faction countdowns of its own, ticking toward what it wants, and some cards let you push one.

Countdowns

A countdown is a die set to a number, ticking toward zero on a trigger: every action roll, every roll with Fear, every rest, every minute that passes in the story. A buyer on the way to the meet is a countdown. Some countdowns run where you can't see them. When a countdown hits zero, the thing happens.

When the crew is working toward something and the world is working against them at once, the GM runs two: a Progress countdown toward what you want and a Consequence countdown toward what you don't. The Rest Of The Rules

Experiences

An Experience is a short phrase about who you've been, at +2. When a roll fits it, spend a Hope before rolling to add the bonus. You can spend on more than one Experience for one roll, a Hope each. You start with two and gain one at levels 2, 5, and 8, and more through advancements.

Helping

Help an Ally: spend a Hope after describing how you help, and the acting player adds a d6 advantage die; several helpers each add one, and only the highest counts. Tag Team: once per session per player, spend 3 Hope to act together with another PC: both describe the combined action, both roll, and you use the better result for the effect while both players get the Hope or the GM gets the Fear as their dice showed. Group action: when the whole crew tries one thing (sneaking a corridor, holding a line), one leader rolls and everyone else makes a reaction roll; each success gives the leader a d6 advantage die, each failure a disadvantage die.

Proficiency And Damage

Proficiency starts at 1 and rises at levels 2, 5, and 8, and through an advancement. It is the number of damage dice you roll with any weapon. On a critical hit, take the maximum of all the dice and add a normal roll on top. Unarmed: your Strength or Finesse, Melee, d4 physical.

Other Damage

Falls and collisions: Very Close, 1d10+3; Close, 1d20+5; farther, 1d20+15 and a Strength Reaction Roll (15) to avoid marking a Stress. Fire, gas, and drowning: 1d10+3 per exposure, and the GM tells you the countdown. Vehicles and drones take damage the same way you do, against their own thresholds.

Hope, Fear, And The Spotlight In Detail

  • Hope caps at 6. Fear caps at 12. Extra is lost.
  • You can't spend Hope on someone else's Experience.
  • When you roll with Fear or fail, the GM gets the spotlight. They can make a move for free, or spend Fear to spotlight more adversaries, to interrupt, or to trigger a feature marked with a Fear cost. When they're done, the spotlight returns to the crew.
  • The GM can also take the spotlight whenever the story calls for it, without spending, when nobody in the crew is acting.
  • Adversaries mark Hit Points against their own thresholds, mark Stress the way you do, and have Experiences the GM can add for a Fear.
  • When a card or a load calls for an adversary to make a reaction roll, the GM rolls a d20 and adds the adversary's attack bonus against the listed Difficulty.

Conditions On Adversaries

Anything you can suffer they can suffer. Hidden, Restrained, Vulnerable, Blinded, Offline, Stuck, Traced. An adversary clears a condition when the GM spotlights it and spends its action on that.

Downtime Projects

A project is a countdown you tick during long rests: a build, an augment installed, a dive arranged, a favor called in, a scar healed by a ripper. The relevant section says how long; the GM sets the rest. One project per long rest per character.

Levels

Section 0 has the full ladder: a domain card every level, two advancements every level, thresholds up by one every level, an Experience and Proficiency at 2, 5, and 8, Path upgrades at tiers 2 and 3, multiclassing from level 5. The GM tells you when you level; usually every two or three sessions.

Death

When you mark your last Hit Point, you choose a death move. Blaze of Glory: one last action that critically succeeds, then you die. Avoid Death: you drop unconscious and the situation gets worse; roll your Hope Die, and if it's your level or lower you take a scar (cross out a Hope slot, permanently). Risk It All: roll the Duality Dice; Hope higher, you stay up and clear that many HP or Stress; Fear higher, you're gone; doubles, you clear everything. Lose your last Hope slot and the character's story ends at the close of the session.

Death Is Real, And You Author It

This city kills people. When it comes for you, the last choice is yours. The crew inherits your gear, and you build a new operator at the party's level, or take over a character the GM has been running. Resurrection isn't a thing here. Soulkiller is, and that's a different conversation.

Stealth, By What Sees You

Four things detect you in this city, and each one is somebody's craft. Eyes (a guard, a camera's field of view, a witness) and the traces you leave (a casing, a footprint, a body) are beaten by the Ghost deck, for Cleaners and Nomads. Loadout And Vault

You can have five cards active at once: your loadout. The rest are in your vault, on your sheet but switched off. During any rest you can rearrange freely. Mid-scene, you can pull a card from the vault into the loadout by marking Stress equal to its recall cost; it replaces a card of your choice. A recall cost of 0 means it swaps for free. Until level 5 you'll rarely have more than five, and it won't matter. After that, the loadout is where your character's shape shows.

Gaining Cards

  • Level 1: two level-1 cards from your domains.
  • Each level after: one card of your level or lower, plus one more if you spend an advancement on it. By level 10 you hold at least 11 of your two domains' 42 cards and run 5.
  • Multiclassing (level 5 and up, both advancements) adds a third domain at half your level, rounded up, plus a second Tradition's class feature and a Path Foundation. You can do it once.
  • Multiclassing is the only way to take cards from outside your two domains.
Domain-Mastery Cards

Each domain has one level-7 card (Iron-Bound, Forge-Wrought, Wire-Bound, and so on) that only works while four or more of your five active cards are from that domain. They reward a loadout built around one thing. A Solo running four Iron cards is a different animal from one running two and two.

Paths

Each Tradition has two Paths. A Path has a Foundation you take at level 1, a Specialization you can take with a Path Upgrade advancement from tier 2, and a Mastery from tier 3. A Path is never just a number. Each one changes how your turn goes: a pool you bank and spend, a person you name and stand in front of, a companion, a second face, a favor count. Two Solos with different Paths and different cards are different people.

What Makes Two Solos Different

Six things, and none of them is the Tradition. Your Path (Hitter or Bodyguard). Your loadout: five active cards from 42, swapped at every rest. Your chrome: a lot of your power lives in your augments, and a Sandevistan Solo and a Gorilla Arms Solo are not the same animal. Your weapon. Your Origin and Experiences: an Experience is a phrase you wrote at +2, and it says who you are better than any feature can, so every Tradition page suggests six. And, if the story hands you one, a Conversion. The Tradition is the job title. Everything else is you.

How Card Levels Scale

Cards of the same level are about equally strong. Higher levels are bigger, not just better: a level 1 card sharpens a scene, a level 5 card changes an encounter, a level 9 or 10 card is the kind of thing the city tells stories about afterward. Identity still matters more than power. Two characters with the same Tradition and five cards should feel like completely different people based on which five they chose and how they run their loadout.

Three Kinds Of Tech

Three Traditions work with technology directly, each on a different layer. The layer decides who can use it, who can attack it, and what it takes to keep it running.

Nomad Tech: Analog

Mechanical systems, hardware you drive by hand, things built in a garage. No software, and nothing wireless to attack. A Hacker can't get into a Nomad's truck because there's nothing to get into. Clan radios are built to beat corporate jamming. The cost: the clans can't make corporate medicine. What they make instead is in Section D.

Techie Tech: Machines That Run Themselves

Drones, robots, automated systems, medical hardware, all running on instruction sets: the orders that tell a machine what to do. A Techie rewrites those orders. A Hacker breaks them. They work on the same machines, and they share a domain: Wire.

Hacker Tech: The Net

The digital layer, top to bottom: quickhacks, networks, and the deep net behind the Blackwall (Section C). Anyone with an interface can be flashed and dive; a Hacker does it for a living. The Hacker's cards are what a professional brings on top of the roll, and the Deep Seer Path is for the one who goes deepest.

Higher Tiers

A Tier 3 weapon isn't a Tier 1 weapon with a bigger number bolted on for money. It's a different class of thing, which is why it's rare and why the GM hands it out slowly. If you find one early, it changes your fights, and it changes who's looking for you.

The Three Lines

Power is the bullet doing the work. Loud, everywhere, cheap to feed, and every Power weapon has a feature of its own.

Tech charges before it fires. Most Tech weapons carry Charging: mark a Stress before you attack, and cover gives you no disadvantage, and you can attack someone you can't see as long as you know where they are. It shoots through the wall. Better Tech pieces do more with the charge; their row says what.

Smart steers the round in the air, and it needs a sensor read on what it is steering at. Every Smart weapon carries Smart-Linked: it runs off your interface and optics, so a pure human can't fire one. Against anything it can read, cover doesn't matter. Anything it can't read (a pure human, clan-built analog gear, a jammer field, a dead zone) you fire at unguided, at disadvantage, and the Jam quickhack stops it firing at all.

Power and Tech work for anybody. Smart is the line you only get with a neural interface, which is one more thing Step 4 decides. Melee weapons, decks and less-lethal gear have no line.

Armor

Thresholds are Major / Severe before you add your level. Score is how many Armor Slots you have. Unarmored, your thresholds are your level and twice your level, and your score is 0. One armor at a time; augments that add to Armor Score stack with it, up to +2 from everything else combined (Section R); a shield’s Barrier counts with your armor instead.

Armor comes in three kinds, and picking one means choosing how much you wear against how freely you move. Each kind comes in all four tiers, so the jacket you start in has a version worth wearing at every tier. Read your tier's column. Power frames (Section D) are a fourth option and replace any of these.

Networks

A network is a place. It has a tier, a Difficulty, things the GM can do with it, and something living in it, the same way a SkyPark or a warehouse does. Getting into one is a scene, and the whole crew is in the scene.

The Two Countdowns

Progress And Consequence

The GM runs a network with two countdowns. Progress is how close you are to what you came for. Consequence is how close the system is to knowing you're there. Every action roll anyone in the crew makes toward the job moves them: a success with Hope ticks Progress 2, a critical ticks it 3, a success with Fear ticks both 1, a failure with Hope ticks Consequence 2, a failure with Fear ticks Consequence 3. When Progress hits zero, you take a prize. When Consequence hits zero, you're Traced: the system knows where your body is, and its response is on the way.

The Face stalling the guard, the Solo moving the van, the Nomad clamping a Wire Ghost to the camera bundle, and the Hacker at the jack are all ticking the same two dice. The hacker's rolls are deck rolls against the network's Difficulty; everyone else rolls whatever makes sense for what they're doing.

You always know where it stands. The GM tells you the Consequence countdown and what each stage means: at the top it's Normal; halfway down it's Alert (a guard walks the floor, the Demon wakes); near the bottom it's Lockdown (doors bolt, the Demon acts without the GM spending Fear); at zero it's Failsafe. Every network behaves in ways you can learn. The GM knows exactly what sets off each stage; you know the stages are there.

There is no global network. The city is hardwired and segmented. To reach a system you're on the site, at one of its entry points, or coming through a Backdoor you hold. Inner and outer systems are usually separate, so reaching a core takes a body as well as a deck, and a rewrite needs the control room, where the system's settings live.

Everyone Has A Way In

  • A body. Finesse against a lock's Difficulty to get through it by hand, Strength to get through it by force. A networked lock notices a failure with Fear.
  • The clan catalog. A Cutter Line through the camera bundle, a Wire Ghost on a sensor cable, a Shunt on the building's feed, a Grey Box on a console (Section D). None of it touches the network; all of it ticks Progress.
  • A face. Credentials or a story. No roll if the person believes you.
  • A Techie. A drone or a door that's autonomous hardware answers to instruction sets, not the network (Direct Command).

Example: The SkyPark Fleet

The park's drones follow fixed rules: protect the park and its guests; treat any unwhitelisted person in the event zones during Alert, any visible weapon, and any aggression as a threat; guests first, then the service core, then public space; escalate Normal, Alert, Maximum Enforcement, Lockdown; blind at the service tunnels at shift change and in camera zone B-3. At combat pace: Hijack one drone (an attack against its Difficulty of 13), or Fleet if you have the load. Loud, expensive, gone after. As a job: get into the security core (tier 3, Difficulty 18, Progress 10, Consequence 5) with the crew, reach the control room, and run Progress down twice for the rewrite: add the crew's biosignatures to the whitelist. The whole fleet treats you as authorized until the next audit. You walk in the front. The drones smile. Nobody saw a hacker.

Quickhacks do the job. Rewrites make the job stop being one.
The Broadcast Layer (NBN)

Network Broadcast News embeds behavioral influence in standard broadcast streams: emotional priming, attention direction, risk-appetite suppression, brand association at a neural level. It is ambient, not targeted, and every PC's interface blocks it. Where it matters is NPC behavior: the civilian who won't believe what they saw, the bystander whose anger doesn't ignite when it should. The street calls it watching the screens. Recognizing it is part of operating in this city.

The Deep

The deep net is the same net, further down: the war zone behind the Blackwall, the pre-crash relics, the places the AIs hold. It's another world, played with the same rules, and only two things change: you go in as a construct, and you come back with memories you didn't choose.

The Flash And The Fridge

A dive is a crew activity. Everyone with a neural interface can be flashed, and everyone who's flashed goes under together, the way professional strike teams do. One person stays with the bodies: the minder, a PC or an NPC, who can pull the plug. A pure human can't be flashed; there's no interface to read. The pure human holds the fridge, and the fridge is a dangerous place: the net has back channels into real-world systems, and someone who knows where you're diving from can come for the bodies.

The crew doesn't own a flash rig. A dive needs a fixer's back room, an Elysian clinic, a net-security contractor's setup, or a rig you stole. Every dive is a job to arrange before it's a job to run.

Two Bodies, One Sheet

While you're under, your body is unconscious on the fridge with your Hit Points, thresholds, and armor. Your construct is you, projected: it uses your own traits, cards, Experiences, and Path. It carries its own Hit Points (your tier plus 3) and 4 Stress, because your body's are back on the fridge. Hope is shared. When the construct marks its last Hit Point it dies and you surface, hard: mark 2 Stress and a Hit Point on the body. When the body takes damage on the fridge, the construct marks a Stress per Hit Point. Death moves belong to the body only. A construct's death is not yours. The first one is formative anyway.

Your Construct

When you're flashed, the rig creates a construct: a complete copy of your neural state, running as data. It thinks like you and has your memories to the moment of the flash. It can do what you can do, no more and no less.

How it looks is yours to sculpt. Your own face, a better one, chrome, light, an animal, a shape with no name: pick it at Session Zero or at the flash, and change it between dives. The look changes nothing on your sheet.

  • Your construct grows when you do. It runs on your sheet, so nothing about it levels on its own.
  • Constructs are data. Copyable, runnable by others. You may meet your own construct running somewhere in the net, operated by someone else or looping on its own.
  • If you die, your construct may survive. Another operator can run it. The original personality is in there. Playing it is an intimacy.

A zone is a network with worse physics. It has a tier, a Difficulty, a Demon or something stranger, and the same two countdowns; in a zone the Consequence countdown is called disorientation, and the minder keeps a second one on the fridge. Frag, And What A Dive Is Worth

Frag

At the end of every dive, before you decide whether to integrate the run's memories or watch them as a recording, the GM asks: "What was the hardest moment of that run for your construct?" The answer shapes how frag shows up if you integrate. Not a stat. A memory that's going to surface at an inconvenient time.

Frag Is An Experience

When you integrate a dive that cost something, the GM may offer you a new Experience drawn from the construct: Been Dead Before +2, Knows The Old Net +2, Doesn't Flinch +2. You can spend a Hope on it like any Experience. It's also a handle the GM can spend a Fear on, the way an adversary spends an Experience, when the construct's memory decides something for you. Your frag count is the number of frag Experiences you carry: 0 Clean, 1 Seasoned, 2 Faceted, 3 Split, 4 Deep Sea, 5 Ghost Walking, 6 Shed. At Shed the character's story ends the way a last scar's does. Your crew notices before you do.

Section DClan-Fab

What the clans build: power frames, the nomad catalog, and scrap crafting. None of it runs corporate software, which is why none of it can be hacked, and why none of it can be bought at a storefront. This is the analog answer to a city that put everything on the network.

What Makes It Different

  • It won't talk to corp tech. No software, and nothing wireless to attack. A quickhack has nothing to grab. Corp scanners read a frame as unknown powered chassis, and read most of the catalog as nothing at all.
  • Street rippers can't service it. Tolerances, connectors, and software handshakes are all wrong. You need a Techie, a clan tech, or the Forge cards.
  • Visible, when it's worn. A frame is a suit. The catalog is mostly small, ugly, and deniable.
  • Its own power. Clan cells, eight hours active, field-swappable, not interchangeable with corp systems.

Chicago 2089 is a private campaign for one table. This document is the player-facing rules layer for that campaign and is not for sale or distribution.

Engine. The dice engine in Section R (Duality Dice, Hope and Fear, Evasion, Armor and thresholds, Stress, rests, death moves, adversary and environment structure) is adapted from Daggerheart, by Darrington Press. Daggerheart is a trademark of Darrington Press, LLC. This is unofficial fan material for one table; it is not endorsed by or affiliated with Darrington Press or Critical Role. Everything built on that engine here is this campaign's own.

Setting. The Cyberpunk world, corporations, and names belong to R. Talsorian Games and CD Projekt Red. Chicago 2089 is set twelve years after the events of Cyberpunk 2077, in a city of our own invention, and takes care not to spoil the game for anyone at the table who hasn't finished it.

Lineage. The Traditions, the cards, the three kinds of tech, the deep net, and the clan-fab tree were first built for Cities Without Number by Kevin Crawford (Sine Nomine Publishing), and the campaign ran on that engine through its design phase. The ideas kept; the dice changed.

Accessibility. This file is built to be read on a tablet in a browser at any size. Use the A− and A+ buttons, or the Large print switch, and the layout follows. If anything in it is hard to read, tell the GM and it gets fixed.

Edition

Duality Edition, September 2089. The complete rules of the game; supersedes the Cities Without Number edition in full. Where this book and the World Primer disagree, this book wins.
